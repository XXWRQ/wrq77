Much of our work does not begin with a precise question. More often, we receive a vague ambition: make the experience better, create more valuable content, or help the team move faster.

## Separate the symptom from the problem

A symptom is an outcome we can observe. A problem is the structure producing that outcome. When the two are mixed together, teams start debating solutions before they share an understanding.

I usually begin with three questions:

- What have we actually observed?
- Who is affected, and in what context?
- What happens if we do nothing?

> Clarity does not mean making everything certain. It means making the next step worth taking.

## Build a lightweight map

Separate known facts, critical assumptions, and open questions. This does not need to become a heavy document; one page can change the quality of the conversation.

### Known facts

Keep only what can be supported by data, interviews, or observed behaviour. Avoid presenting interpretation as fact.

### Critical assumptions

Name the beliefs that would change the direction if they proved false. Test these before researching every unknown equally.

## Choose the smallest useful test

A good next step has three qualities: its cost is contained, it creates new information, and its outcome will change a later decision.

```text
Symptom → Problem map → Critical assumption → Small test → Next decision
```

The method does not remove uncertainty. It helps a team keep moving while uncertainty is still present.
